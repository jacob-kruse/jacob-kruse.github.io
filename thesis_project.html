Thesis Project

Introduction

    At the University of Georgia, I obtained my Master of Science degree in Computer Science. During my time at UGA, I worked as Graduate Research Assistant in the THINC Lab. My research focused primarily on reinforcement learning, a machine learning paradigm that allows agents to learn through interactions with its environment. This research combined with my robotics background led me to develop a reinforcement learning framework that could be effectively applied to physical systems. I developed this framework over the course of a year, along with the physical robotic ecosystem where it was applied. As shown in Figure 1, I utilized a system consisting of KUKA collaborative arm with an Ubiros gripper for manipulation and an RGB-D camera for perception. Applying my reinforcement learning framework, I was able to teach the robot how to sort recyclable objects in its workspace.

Publishing

    The findings from this project were non-trivial, which led myself, my advisor, and another professor to try and publish the work. Initially, we strode to publish the results in the 2026 offering of the Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL). With the paper submission deadline approaching, the method had been clearly and effectively laid out in our paper and the simulation experiments had been completed, meaning all that was left to do was run the experiments on the physical system. If you have any experience with robotics, you know that nothing ever goes according to plan (we all have that one demo that still haunts us), which is exactly what happened during this period. About two weeks before the paper submission deadline, I began the long and tedious physical experiments. After the first few rounds of tests, the results were not as expected, exhibiting that my method performed almost exactly the same as the baselines. No problem I thought, more data will reveal the behavior I saw in simulation and with previous physical testing. However, after running even more tests, the results did not change. This was strange and perplexing; why was the data behaving so strange? Now, you might say that I was being a bad researcher; why am I trying to elicit desirable results for my method? Altough this may be true, after much pondeirng and further experimentation, I figured out what was occurring. The problem was realtively simple: the current experimental setup would not reveal any difference in the methods. To fix this, I simply changed the neural network intitialization to produce enough variance in the initial actions for each seed (See Section ...). This change fixed the problem I was encountering, so in the end, I would say I was being a good researcher (Nobel Prize please and thanks).

    At this point, I had only a week left to complete the experiments. On top of this, I had to start from scratch due to the change I made to the experimental setup. Determined to complete these experiments, I continued on with increased effort; but another problem arose shortly after. While running experiments on Memorial Day of May 25th 2026 (my day off mind you), the robot executed an action at an unorthodox approach angle. During its trajectory, the power supply connection on the gripper collided with the base of the robot, completely rending the connection and leaving the gripper unable to function. If you used that handy-dandy link for the 2026 CoRL conference above, you will see that the deadline for the full paper submission was May 28, only a couple of days away. I am sure you can imagine my reaction -- pure panic; the physical experiments I needed to complete both the CoRL paper and my thesis were no longer able to conducted. Would I be able to publish the paper? How was this going to affect my graduation? Problem solving mode activated: Luckily, I was able to contact the company that produced the gripper, a small startup. After talking with the CEO (like I said a startup), we were able to expedite the necessary parts and I was able to repair the gripper by midday of May 27. I was then able to grind out the remaining experiments by the afternoon of May 28. The rest of the night was spent on preparing the paper for submission. If you ever meet my roommate Matt Dettweiler, make sure to ask him about this night (I will leave it at that). After an extremely long and stressful week of running experiments and polishing the paper, I submitted it to CoRL late that evening. How exciting!

    If you are unfamiliar with the publishing process, after submitting your paper, it has to be reviewed. The paper is assigned a number of reviewers who leave comments, formulate questions, and ultimately provide an opinion of whether or not the paper should be accepted. This process generally takes around a month so the reviewers have time to understand the paper and provide meaningful feedback for the work. The reviews for CoRL opened on August 4th, a day I would like to say I had been excited for and looking forward to; but, I didn't even realize my paper had finished the review process until I received an email: "[CoRL 2026] Decision notification for your submission ... After careful consideration, we regret to infrom you that your paper was not selected for acceptance this year". All that stress and hard work seemingly for nothing, all without a chance to rebute the reviewers; academia can be cruel. Funny enough, I agreed with most of the reviewers' comments: not enough experimental data, misleading title, and results did not isolate each part of the framework. Additionally, it was stated that 36% of papers received a straight rejection in the first round due to the high volume of submissions. Furthermore, I came across some Nvidia papers that were being publsihed to CoRL sometime after, each of which had incredible presentation, method, and results. All of this was in my mind and I couldn't help thinking: no wonder my paper was rejected. This might sound extremely pessimistic, but it helped me realize some things.

    First, I should not be comparing myself to a lot of these other contributors like Nvidia. Looking at the number of people involved in the work and considering the resources available to them, it goes without saying that they are at a significant advantage.
